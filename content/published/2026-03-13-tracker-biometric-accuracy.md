# Does your tracker actually know your heart rate

Most fitness trackers use light to measure blood flow. This process is called photoplethysmography. It is convenient but often inaccurate. Sweat and movement interfere with the signal. If your data is wrong, your training will be wrong. You need a device that handles these variables.

![Heart rate monitoring](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1526506118085-60ce8714f8c5?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1200&q=80)

## The signal to noise problem

Motion artifacts are the biggest challenge for wrist based sensors. When you run, your arm moves. This creates noise in the data. Cheap sensors cannot distinguish between your pulse and your movement. They often report a heart rate that matches your cadence. This lead to overtraining. High quality hardware uses advanced algorithms to filter this noise. Accurate data requires a stable connection between the sensor and your skin.

## Why HRV is the ultimate metric

Heart rate is just the baseline. Heart rate variability or HRV tells the real story. HRV measures the variation in time between each heartbeat. It reflects the state of your autonomic nervous system. A high HRV means you are recovered. A low HRV means you are under stress. Most trackers only measure heart rate during exercise. You need a device that tracks HRV during sleep. This provides a clear picture of your readiness for the day.

## Using data for performance

Data is useless without action. Do not just look at the numbers. Use them to adjust your intensity. If your recovery score is low, take a rest day. If your HRV is high, push your limits. Professional athletes use biometrics to avoid injury. You can do the same. The right tool turns raw numbers into a competitive advantage.
